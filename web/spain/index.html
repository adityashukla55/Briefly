Official letters from Spain, explained in English

Spanish offices send formal letters with strict deadlines, often counted in working days (días hábiles). Many letters now arrive online as electronic notifications: they count as delivered when you open them, or after 10 calendar days if you don’t.

Offices that write to you

  • Agencia Tributaria (AEAT) — Tax agency. The state tax office, often called Hacienda. It handles income tax (IRPF), VAT (IVA) and tax refunds.
  • Tesorería General de la Seguridad Social (TGSS) — Social Security treasury. Registers workers and collects Social Security payments (cotizaciones), for example the monthly fee of self-employed people (autónomos).
  • Instituto Nacional de la Seguridad Social (INSS) — Social Security benefits office. Pays pensions, sick-leave and birth benefits, and the minimum income (ingreso mínimo vital).
  • Servicio Público de Empleo Estatal (SEPE) — State employment service. Pays unemployment benefit (prestación por desempleo) and unemployment allowances (subsidios).
  • Oficina de Extranjería / Policía Nacional — Immigration office. Handles NIE numbers, residence permits and TIE cards for foreigners. Fingerprints and cards are done at Policía Nacional offices.
  • Ayuntamiento — Town hall. Your local council. It handles address registration (padrón), local taxes like IBI and plusvalía, and local fines.
  • Dirección General de Tráfico (DGT) — Traffic authority. Handles driving licences, vehicle records and traffic fines on most roads.
  • Organismo de recaudación / Diputación — Local tax collection office. Collects local taxes and fines for many town halls, for example IBI, car tax and parking fines.
  • Agencia tributaria autonómica — Regional tax agency. Your region’s tax office. It handles taxes on inheritances, gifts and property purchases (ITP). In the Basque Country and Navarra it also handles income tax.
  • Juzgado / Tribunal — Court. A court. Court letters always matter — never ignore them, and get advice quickly.
  • Notificaciones electrónicas (DEHú / Carpeta Ciudadana) — Electronic notifications. The government website where official letters wait for you online. It isn’t the office that wrote the letter — open the letter to see who sent it.
  • Arrendador / inmobiliaria — Landlord or letting agency. The owner of your flat, or the agency that manages it.
  • Compañía de luz, gas o agua — Energy or water company. The company that sells you electricity, gas or water.
  • Comunidad de propietarios — Owners’ association. The association of all flat owners in a building. It shares costs like cleaning, lifts and repairs.
  • Banco / empresa de recobro — Bank or debt collector. Your bank, or a company that collects debts for others. Check that the debt is real before you pay.

Common kinds of letters

  • Providencia de apremio — Enforced collection notice. You didn’t pay in time. A surcharge is now added, and if you still don’t pay they can take the money from your bank account, wages or property.
  • Diligencia de embargo — Seizure notice. They are taking money or property to pay a debt — for example from your bank account, wages or a tax refund.
  • Requerimiento de pago (procedimiento monitorio) — Court payment order. A court orders you to pay a debt. You usually have 20 working days to pay or to oppose it. If you do nothing, they can take the money by force.
  • Reintegro / cobro indebido — Request to pay money back. They believe you got money you weren’t entitled to, for example unemployment benefit, and want it back.
  • Multa / sanción — Fine. An official fine, for example for a traffic or parking offence. You can usually pay with a discount or object — not both.
  • Reclamación de deuda / burofax — Payment demand. Someone says you owe money and asks you to pay. If you ignore it, the next step can be a court case or a list of people who owe money (fichero de morosos).
  • Propuesta / trámite de alegaciones — Proposal — chance to explain. A draft decision, not the final one. You can reply with your arguments and proof (alegaciones) before they decide.
  • Citación / cita — Appointment. They want you to come in person on a set date. Missing it without a good reason can delay or harm your case — or cut your unemployment benefit.
  • Requerimiento de documentación — Request for documents. They need papers or information from you. If you don’t answer in time, they may close your application or decide without your papers.
  • Resolución denegatoria — Rejection. Your application was turned down. You can usually appeal — the end of the letter says how and by when.
  • Liquidación provisional — Tax assessment. The tax office checked your tax and set the amount. You may have to pay more — or get money back.
  • Resolución estimatoria / concesión — Approval. Your application was approved. The letter shows what you get, from when and for how long.
  • Desahucio / resolución de contrato — Termination or eviction notice. Someone is ending a contract — for example your rental contract, your job or a service. Eviction (desahucio) and dismissal (despido) letters have short deadlines.
  • Factura de luz, gas o agua — Energy or water bill. A bill for electricity, gas or water. Check the period, the meter readings and whether the reading was estimated (lectura estimada).

Questions

How long do I have to answer an official letter in Spain?

It depends on the letter. Look for words like “plazo”, “hasta el”, “días hábiles” — they mark the deadline. In Spain, deadlines usually count from the day after the letter is delivered (notificación). A paper letter is delivered on the day you receive it; an electronic one when you open it, or 10 calendar days after it was made available. We estimate 3 days for post — use the real date if you know it. To object, the letter usually names the way to do it (“recurso de reposición”, “reclamación económico-administrativa”, “recurso de alzada”, “reclamación previa”, “alegaciones”, “oposición”, “recurso”).
