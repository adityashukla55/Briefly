Official letters from the United Kingdom, explained in English

In the UK, many deadlines count from the date printed on the letter, and some are very short — for example 14 days to answer a court claim. Rules can differ between England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, so always check the dates and instructions in your letter.

Offices that write to you

  • HM Revenue and Customs (HMRC) — Tax office. The UK tax office. It handles Income Tax, National Insurance, VAT, Child Benefit and tax credits.
  • Department for Work and Pensions (DWP) — Benefits office. Pays most benefits, for example Universal Credit, Personal Independence Payment (PIP), State Pension and Employment and Support Allowance.
  • Council (borough, district or city council) — Local council. Your local council. It collects council tax, deals with housing benefit and council tax support, and gives out many parking tickets.
  • Driver and Vehicle Licensing Agency (DVLA) — Driver and vehicle office. Deals with driving licences, vehicle tax and the vehicle logbook (V5C).
  • TV Licensing — TV licence office. Collects the TV licence fee for the BBC. You need a licence to watch or record live TV on any channel, or to use BBC iPlayer.
  • Home Office / UK Visas and Immigration (UKVI) — Immigration office. Decides on visas, settlement (indefinite leave to remain) and citizenship applications.
  • County Court / Magistrates’ Court / Tribunal (HMCTS) — Court or tribunal. A court or tribunal. Court papers are always important — never ignore them.
  • Enforcement agents (bailiffs) — Bailiffs. Collect debts after a court order, for example for council tax, parking fines or a CCJ. They can add fees and, in some cases, take goods.
  • Debt collection agency — Debt collector. A company that collects debts for others, or that bought an old debt. Check that the debt is really yours and the amount is right before you pay.
  • Police / Safety Camera Partnership — Police (speeding and driving). Sends letters about speeding, red lights and other driving offences caught by cameras or officers.
  • Private parking operator — Private parking company. Companies that run car parks for shops, hospitals and landlords. Their Parking Charge Notice is a bill under a contract, not a fine from the police or council.
  • Energy supplier — Gas or electricity supplier. The company that sells you gas or electricity.
  • Water company — Water company. Supplies water and takes away waste water. Water companies can’t cut off water to homes for unpaid bills.
  • Landlord / letting agent — Landlord or letting agent. The owner of your home, or the agency that manages it.
  • NHS — Health service. The National Health Service. It sends appointment letters, test results and, in some cases, bills or penalty charges.
  • Student Loans Company (SLC) — Student loan office. Pays student finance and collects repayments, usually through your salary once you earn enough.

Common kinds of letters

  • Notice of enforcement (bailiffs) — Enforcement notice. Enforcement agents (bailiffs) will collect the debt. They add fees at each stage, and can visit your home.
  • County Court claim form — Court claim against you. Someone is taking you to court for money. You must answer within 14 days, or the court can decide against you without a hearing.
  • Single Justice Procedure notice — Charge for a minor offence. You are charged with a minor offence, for example no TV licence, speeding or not paying a train fare. A magistrate decides on paper unless you ask for a hearing.
  • Reminder / final notice — Payment reminder. A payment is late. If you don’t pay, the next step is often court action or losing the right to pay in instalments.
  • Letter of claim / letter before action — Warning before court. They say they will take you to court if you don’t pay or reply. For debts, you usually have 30 days to reply with the reply form.
  • Overpayment letter — Request to pay money back. They say they paid you too much, for example Universal Credit, housing benefit or tax credits, and want the money back.
  • Penalty Charge Notice (PCN) — Parking or traffic fine (council). A fine from the council for parking, a bus lane or a traffic rule. Paying within 14 days usually halves it.
  • Parking Charge Notice (private) — Private parking charge. A private car park company says you broke its rules. It’s a bill under a contract, not a fine — but it can end in court if you ignore it.
  • Notice of Intended Prosecution (NIP) — Speeding notice — name the driver. A camera or officer caught a vehicle speeding or running a red light. The keeper must say who was driving within 28 days.
  • Penalty notice (HMRC) — Tax penalty. A penalty for a late tax return or late payment. You can appeal within 30 days if you had a good reason.
  • P800 / Simple Assessment — Tax calculation. HMRC worked out your tax for a year. It shows if you paid too much (a refund) or too little (tax to pay).
  • Decision letter (benefits) — Benefit decision. A decision about your benefit — for example what you get, a change, a sanction or that it stops.
  • Refusal letter — Refusal. Your application was refused. The letter says if you can challenge it, how and by when.
  • Approval / grant letter — Approval. Your application was approved. Check that names, dates and amounts are right.
  • Appointment letter — Appointment. They want you to come to an appointment, for example at the Jobcentre, an assessment, an interview or a hospital. Missing it can delay or reduce your benefits or case.
  • Notice seeking possession / notice to quit — Eviction notice. Your landlord wants you to leave. The notice must follow strict rules — many notices are not valid.
  • Council tax bill — Council tax bill. Your council tax for the year, based on your home’s band. It is usually paid in 10 monthly instalments.
  • Energy or water bill — Energy or water bill. A bill for gas, electricity or water. It shows what you used and when to pay.

Questions

How long do I have to answer an official letter in the United Kingdom?

It depends on the letter. Look for words like “by”, “within”, “no later than” — they mark the deadline. In the UK, a letter sent by first class post is usually treated as delivered on the second working day after it was posted. But many deadlines, for example for appeals, count from the date printed on the letter. To object, the letter usually names the way to do it (“mandatory reconsideration”, “administrative review”, “representations”, “challenge”, “appeal”, “dispute”, “review”).
