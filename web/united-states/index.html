Official letters from the United States, explained in English

In the US, many deadlines count from the date printed on the notice, not from the day it arrives — so open official mail right away. Rules often differ from state to state, so always check the dates and instructions in your letter.

Offices that write to you

  • Internal Revenue Service (IRS) — Federal tax agency. The federal tax office. It handles income tax returns, refunds and tax debts.
  • State Department of Revenue or Taxation — State tax agency. Your state’s tax office. Most states have their own income tax, with their own forms and deadlines.
  • Social Security Administration (SSA) — Social Security office. Pays retirement, disability and survivor benefits, and SSI for older or disabled people with low income. It also issues Social Security numbers.
  • U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) — Immigration services. Decides on green cards, work permits, citizenship and other immigration applications.
  • County or State Court — Court. A court. Court papers are always important — never ignore them.
  • Debt collection agency — Debt collector. A company that collects debts for others, or that bought an old debt. Check that the debt is really yours before you pay.
  • Landlord / property management — Landlord or property manager. The owner of your home, or the company that manages it.
  • Utility company — Electric, gas or water company. The company that sells you electricity, gas or water.
  • Health insurer / Medicaid / Marketplace — Health insurance. Your health insurance company, or a public program like Medicaid or a Marketplace plan from HealthCare.gov or your state.
  • Department of Human Services / Social Services — Social services (food and cash help). Your state or county office for SNAP (food help), cash help and often Medicaid.
  • State unemployment insurance agency — Unemployment office. Your state office that pays unemployment benefits after you lose your job.
  • Student loan servicer — Student loan company. The company that collects your student loan payments, for the government or a private lender.
  • County Assessor / Tax Collector — Property tax office. Your county or city office that values homes and land and collects property tax.
  • Department of Motor Vehicles (DMV) — Driver and vehicle office. Your state office for driver’s licenses, ID cards and car registration. Some states call it RMV, BMV or MVD.
  • Police / parking enforcement — Parking and traffic tickets. Gives out parking and traffic tickets (citations) for the city, county or state.

Common kinds of letters

  • Notice of intent to levy / garnishment — Enforcement notice. They plan to take money by force, or have started — for example from your bank account, wages or benefits (a levy or garnishment).
  • Summons and complaint — Court case against you. Someone is suing you. You must file a written answer with the court by the deadline, or you can lose automatically.
  • Balance due / past-due notice — Payment reminder. You owe money that was due before, for example tax or a bill. Interest, penalties or late fees can grow until you pay.
  • Debt validation notice — Letter from a debt collector. A debt collector says you owe money. You can dispute the debt in writing by the date in the notice — you get at least 30 days.
  • Proposed changes to your tax return (CP2000) — Proposed tax change. Your tax return doesn’t match what employers, banks or others reported. They propose a change to your tax. Respond by the date in the notice.
  • Change to your return or refund (CP11, CP12, CP49) — Changed tax return or refund. They corrected your tax return, or used your refund to pay a debt. Your refund or the tax you owe is different from what you expected.
  • Notice of overpayment — Request to pay money back. They say they paid you too much, for example Social Security or unemployment benefits, and want the money back.
  • Request for Evidence (RFE) / Notice of Intent to Deny (NOID) — Request for more proof. USCIS needs more proof before it decides your case. A Notice of Intent to Deny means they plan to say no unless your answer changes their mind.
  • Request for information — Request for documents. They need papers or answers from you before they can decide, or to keep your benefits or coverage going.
  • Denial notice — Denial. Your application or claim was turned down. You can usually appeal — the letter says how and by when.
  • Approval notice — Approval. Your application was approved. Check that names, dates and amounts are right.
  • Appointment / interview notice — Appointment. They want you to come to an appointment, for example for biometrics or an interview. Missing it can delay or end your case or your benefits.
  • Jury summons — Jury duty. You were picked at random as a possible juror. You must answer the summons, even if you can’t serve.
  • Parking or traffic ticket (citation) — Ticket. A fine for breaking a parking or traffic rule. Pay it or contest it by the date on the ticket — if you do nothing, it usually costs more.
  • Eviction notice / notice to pay rent or quit — Eviction notice. Your landlord wants you to pay or move out. Notice periods are set by state law — for unpaid rent often only 3 to 14 days.

Questions

How long do I have to answer an official letter in the United States?

It depends on the letter. Look for words like “respond by”, “pay by”, “no later than”, “within”, “deadline” — they mark the deadline. In the US, many deadlines count from the date printed on the notice, not from the day it arrives. Some offices add a few days for mail (Social Security assumes 5 days), and court deadlines usually start on the day you were served. To object, the letter usually names the way to do it (“appeal”, “request for reconsideration”, “dispute”, “protest”, “hearing request”).
